What to carry, and how to behave

Documents — carry two sets, in one folder

  • 3 copies of your resume, 4 passport photos, a pen
  • 10th and 12th marksheets, diploma/degree certificates, all originals plus one photocopy set
  • Aadhaar, PAN if you have it, and a bank passbook copy for salary account
  • Experience or internship letter, if any; registration certificate (Nursing Council, State Pharmacy Council, ITI NCVT)
  • Caste/EWS certificate if you are claiming reservation — for government posts this is checked strictly

Body language — the part nobody marks you on but everyone notices

DO
  • Reach 15 minutes early; sit down and breathe before you go in
  • Knock, ask before entering, greet the panel — "Good morning sir/ma'am"
  • Sit upright, hands on your lap or on the table, both feet on the floor
  • Look at the person asking the question; if there are three, glance at all three
  • Speak slowly. Pause before answering — it reads as confidence
  • Say "I don't know, but I would find out by…" instead of guessing
DON'T
  • Don't keep your phone even on silent — switch it off before entering
  • Don't cross your arms, tap the table or bounce your leg
  • Don't chew gum, don't lean back into the chair, don't look at the floor
  • Don't say "any job is fine" — it sounds like you have no interest
  • Don't criticise your last employer or college
  • Don't ask about salary in the first round unless they raise it

Answers that work, even with no experience

  • "Tell me about yourself" — 45 seconds: who you are, what you studied, one thing you did with it, why this job. Not your family history.
  • "Why should we hire you?" — name the one thing the job needs and show you have it: "You need someone who can handle 60 samples a day without mixing labels — that was my daily work in my internship."
  • "What is your weakness?" — a real one plus what you do about it. "I used to be slow at documentation, so I made a checklist; my last supervisor said my records were the cleanest."
  • "You have no experience" — agree, then point at practice: college labs, internships, a project, a certificate, a portfolio.
  • Ask them two questions — "What does a normal shift look like?" and "What would you want me to learn in the first month?"

Salary — how to negotiate without losing the offer

Before the conversation

  • Find the range first. Ask two people already doing the job, and look at what the same role pays in your city. A number you cannot defend is worse than no number.
  • Know the difference between CTC and in-hand. A ₹25,000 CTC can mean ₹21,000 in hand after PF and professional tax. Always ask: "What is the monthly in-hand?"
  • Decide your floor before you walk in — the number below which you will say no. Write it down. Do not move it in the room.

What to say

  • If they ask your expectation first: "I am looking at the market range for a DMLT fresher in Ahmedabad, which I understand is around ₹18,000 to ₹24,000. Where would this role sit?" — a range, never a single number, and you have handed the question back.
  • If the offer is low: "Thank you, I am keen on this role. I was hoping for closer to ₹22,000 given the shift work. Is there room to move?" — keen, specific, one question.
  • If they say the budget is fixed: ask for what is not budget — earlier increment review, a shift allowance, transport, food, a confirmed designation, or a shorter probation.
  • Never accept on the spot. "Can I have the offer in writing and confirm by tomorrow?" is always acceptable.

Negotiable even for freshers

  • Shift allowance and night-shift food/transport
  • Joining bonus or a relocation amount
  • Probation length and the increment month
  • Designation on the letter
  • Start date, so you can finish exams

Walk away from these

  • A "training bond" with a penalty if you leave
  • Salary paid in cash with no payslip
  • No PF or ESI where the law requires it
  • First month "held" as security
  • Any fee to join — that is a scam, not a job

Get it in writing

  • The offer letter must state: fixed pay, allowances, deductions, working hours, weekly off, leave, notice period and probation.
  • If they promise something in the interview that is not in the letter, ask for it by email — "as discussed, please confirm the night allowance of ₹2,000". An email reply is proof.
  • For daily or piece-rate work, a WhatsApp message with the rate and payment date is your contract. Keep it.

If you face harassment at work

This is general information, not legal advice. If you are in danger right now, call 112. For free legal help, your District Legal Services Authority (DLSA) gives a lawyer at no cost under the Legal Services Authorities Act — find yours at nalsa.gov.in.

First, three things that protect you later

  • Write it down the same day. Date, time, place, what was said or done, who saw it. A plain notebook or a note on your phone is enough, and a pattern is far stronger than one incident.
  • Put complaints in writing. Email HR or your manager instead of only speaking. Bcc your personal email address so the record survives even if you lose access to the work account.
  • Keep the evidence. Screenshot messages before they are deleted, save call logs, keep your appointment letter and payslips. Do not hand over your only copy to anyone.

Sexual harassment — the law is on your side (POSH Act, 2013)

  • It covers unwelcome physical contact, demands for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct.
  • Every workplace with 10 or more employees must have an Internal Committee (IC) — ask HR in writing for the names of its members and its contact.
  • Where there are fewer than 10 employees, or the complaint is against the employer, you go to the Local Committee (LC) at the district level. The District Officer's office will tell you where it sits.
  • File within 3 months of the incident — the committee can extend it by another 3 months if there was a genuine reason for the delay.
  • The inquiry must be completed in 90 days. You can ask for interim relief such as transfer or paid leave during the inquiry.
  • You cannot be victimised for complaining. Retaliation — a transfer, a bad appraisal, being pushed out — is itself a violation you can report.
  • A colleague or bystander can also file the complaint, and NCW accepts complaints online if your workplace does nothing.

Mental harassment, bullying and "office politics" that crosses a line

  • There is no single bullying law in India, so you use the routes that exist: your company's grievance or POSH policy, your HR in writing, and your state Labour Commissioner if the employer is covered by the Shops and Establishments Act or the Factories Act.
  • Shouting, public humiliation, threats, forced overtime without pay, withholding salary, or forcing you to resign are all worth reporting — the last two are wage and employment violations, not just bad manners.
  • Central government employees can use CPGRAMS; state government employees have their own grievance portals.
  • If you are being pushed to resign, do not sign anything on the spot. Ask for it in writing and get advice first — a resignation letter can end your claim.

Physical assault or threats

  • Call 112 and get a medical examination done the same day — the injury report is evidence.
  • You can file an FIR at the police station with jurisdiction over the workplace. You are entitled to a free copy of the FIR.
  • Tell your family or a friend where you are going and when; do not go alone to an isolated site to "sort it out".
  • Report it to the employer in writing the same day, even if you also go to the police — the employer's failure to act becomes their liability.

Where to call

  • 112 — police and emergency, anywhere in India
  • 181 — women's helpline, for harassment, violence and counselling
  • NCW — online complaint at ncwapps.nic.in if the workplace does not act
  • DLSA / NALSA — free lawyer, nalsa.gov.in, or the district court in your city
  • State Labour Commissioner — unpaid salary, forced resignation, safety violations

How to check a company yourself, in five minutes

  • Company exists? Search the name on the MCA portal — every registered Indian company is listed there with its CIN and directors.
  • Real address? Put the address into Google Maps. A hospital or factory has photos and years of reviews. A scam has a flat number.
  • Real website? A working domain with a phone number and a physical address. "Coming soon" pages and free subdomains are a warning sign.
  • Who contacted you? A company recruiter uses a company email domain, not a personal Gmail or a Telegram-only account.
  • Ask one specific question — "who is the reporting manager and what is the shift timing?" Scammers cannot answer specifics.
